Since April 2024, Iran has fired more than 870 ballistic missiles and 1,170 drones directly at Israel — the first time in history it has done so. Israel struck back across 24 of 31 Iranian provinces. Then the United States joined in.

By StrikeWatch Data Desk March 5, 2026 Sources: IDF · IRGC · Al Jazeera · Reuters · Wikipedia · FDD

It began with a consulate. On April 1, 2024, Israeli aircraft struck the Iranian embassy annex in Damascus, killing two IRGC generals and five officers. Iran had long maintained that attacking its diplomatic soil meant attacking Iran itself.

Thirteen days later, more than 300 projectiles crossed Iranian airspace toward Israel. What had for decades been a "shadow war" — fought through proxies, assassinations, and cyberattacks — had become something new: direct, open, nation-state warfare.

This is the story of how it escalated, weapon by weapon, strike by strike, over the two years that followed.

The Region · Before

A conflict fought in the shadows — until it wasn't

For decades, Iran and Israel waged a covert war. Israel killed Iranian nuclear scientists. Iran armed Hezbollah and Hamas. Both sides struck each other's ships, pipelines, and computer networks. Neither ever fired directly at the other's soil.

The map shows the two countries separated by roughly 1,700 km — and by Iraq, Syria, and Jordan in between.

April 13–14, 2024

Operation True Promise I: The largest drone strike in history

Just after midnight, Iran launched 170 Shahed drones, 30 cruise missiles, and 120 ballistic missiles at Israel — roughly 60 tonnes of explosive material in a single salvo. It was the first direct Iranian attack on Israeli soil in history.

A coalition of US, British, French, and Jordanian forces helped Israel's Arrow-3 and Iron Dome systems. By dawn, the IDF announced it had intercepted 99% of all incoming weapons. A seven-year-old Bedouin girl was struck by falling debris and critically injured.

Cost asymmetry: Iran spent ~$80–100M on the attack. Defending it cost the coalition roughly $1 billion — a 10:1 ratio in Iran's favour, exposing a long-term flaw in missile defence economics.
April 18, 2024

Israel replies — with surgical restraint

Israel's response was a single, deliberate strike. An explosion near Isfahan destroyed an air-defence radar specifically protecting the Natanz nuclear facility. No mass retaliation, no attempt to escalate.

The message was unmistakable: We can reach your nuclear sites whenever we choose.

October 1, 2024

True Promise II: Hypersonic. Faster. Harder to stop.

Iran had learned from April. It discarded the slow drones and cruise missiles and fired approximately 200 ballistic missiles — including the hypersonic Fattah-1 and Kheibar Shekan — in multiple waves toward Israeli airbases.

The triggers: Haniyeh assassinated in Tehran in July; Nasrallah killed in Beirut in September; IRGC commander Abbas Nilforoushan killed alongside him. Iran named the operation a response to all three.

Interception rate fell to ~90%. Twenty to thirty-two missiles struck the Nevatim Airbase — real visible damage on Israeli soil. Several more hit Tel Nof Airbase and a school in Gedera.
October 26, 2024

Israel destroys Iran's air defences

Israel's counter-strike was not about retaliation — it was preparation. Israeli aircraft systematically destroyed nearly all of Iran's Russian-supplied S-300 air-defence batteries, the shields protecting its nuclear facilities.

Analysts called it a "key removal" operation: not a response, but the deliberate clearing of obstacles for what would come next.

Strategic shift: Iran had lost its main layer of protection. Every Iranian nuclear site was now exposed to direct Israeli air attack.
June 13, 2025

Operation Rising Lion: Israel strikes first — and hard

In the early hours of June 13, with the IAEA warning that Iran held enough enriched uranium for nine nuclear warheads, Israel launched its most ambitious offensive in history. More than 1,000 air force sorties, 600 refuelling operations, strikes across 24 of 31 Iranian provinces.

The Natanz enrichment facility was hit. Missile programme chief General Hajizadeh was killed in his command post. Targets included military infrastructure, air defences, drone factories, and IRGC headquarters.

June 13–24, 2025

The Twelve-Day War: 1,550 projectiles in 12 days

Iran's response was the most intense missile barrage of the entire conflict. Over twelve days, it fired approximately 550 ballistic missiles and 1,000 drones. The Haifa oil refinery was hit. A residential building in northern Israel collapsed. 28 Israelis were killed.

A US-brokered ceasefire came into effect on June 24. Iran fired two final missiles with two hours remaining on the clock. Both were intercepted.

June 22: US B-2 bombers from Diego Garcia struck three Iranian nuclear sites. Iran fired back at the US base in Qatar. Trump declared the Iranian nuclear programme "obliterated."
February 28 – March 5, 2026

"Accelerating, not decelerating": The US joins in

On February 27, 2026, the IAEA revealed Iran had secretly stored highly enriched uranium in a hidden facility that survived the June strikes. The next day, the United States and Israel launched the largest combined military operation against Iran in history.

The Israeli army chief of staff, defence minister, and dozens of IRGC commanders were killed. On March 1, Supreme Leader Khamenei was killed. Iran fired back at 27 US military bases across 9 countries.

Iran's missile rate fell 86% by Day 6 — launchers were being destroyed faster than they could be replaced, per US Joint Chiefs chairman General Dan Caine.
The Toll · March 5, 2026

More than 1,200 dead. The war continues.

At least 1,190 people have been killed in Iran by Israeli and American strikes since April 2024. Twenty-eight Israelis died. Six American soldiers were killed in Iranian retaliatory strikes on regional bases.

Iran's economy has collapsed. The rial trades at 1.45 million per dollar. Protests have erupted in more than 100 cities — the largest unrest since 1979. The conflict is ongoing.

As of March 5, 2026, the United States and Israel are still striking Iran. Iran is still firing back — at roughly 14% of its peak rate. The reason is mechanical: its launchers are being destroyed faster than they can be replaced.

Iran's air defences are gone. Its IRGC command structure has been decapitated. Its supreme leader is dead. Its nuclear programme may be set back by years. And yet the missiles keep coming.

A third of all oil exported by sea still passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Shipping lanes have shifted. Fuel prices have climbed. The cost of a war, as always, spreads far beyond the combatants.

Two countries that spent four decades fighting each other through proxies are now fighting each other directly — with weapons, in the open, over the same skies. The question the world is asking is no longer whether this will end. It is what will be left when it does.
